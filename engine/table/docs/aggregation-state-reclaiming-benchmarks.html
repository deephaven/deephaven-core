Aggregation state reclaiming: JMH results

Incremental keyed aggregations now remove groups that empty from their hash table and release the storage of fully empty 2048-position blocks; an optional collapse also packs sparse blocks together. These results compare upstream main with the PR in each mode, so you can see where reclaiming saves time, memory and cycle latency, and what it costs when groups return.

Measured
AggregationIncrementalBenchmark: batches of 900 update cycles of a keyed sumBy. 1M rows with 10k added and removed per cycle, and 10M rows with 100k per cycle (48 GB heap).
Method
Two JVM forks, each with three warmup and five measured batches. Time is JMH's score; error bars are JMH's 99.9% intervals over the ten measured batches.
Machine
Intel i9-14900KS (hybrid P/E cores) in an LXC container, 24 logical CPUs online, 128 GB, performance governor, turbo boost enabled, otherwise idle.
Commits
Main at ; the PR at .

Configurations

  • Main: upstream main, which predates reclaiming. The baseline; every percentage is relative to it.
  • none: the PR without reclaiming, which isolates its other improvements to the update cycle and the adaptive recyclers of #8698.
  • Blocks: releaseBlocks(1), the default. Releases empty blocks and never moves a state.
  • Collapse 0.75: releaseBlocks(0.75), which also collapses runs of sparse blocks.
  • Collapse 0.5: releaseBlocks(0.5).
  • Main again: main rerun after every other configuration, to show drift over the run.

Workloads

  • Add only: rows are only added, each with a new group.
  • Sliding window: each cycle removes the oldest rows, so groups empty in the order they were created.
  • Random churn: each cycle removes rows chosen at random, so blocks thin out unevenly.
  • Rows per group: 1 or 100 consecutive rows. With one, every removal empties a group and every addition creates one; with 100, most changes touch groups that already exist. The 10M-row runs use one row per group only.
  • Groups return: a group's key comes back after its state has been removed; the keys cycle through a space twice the number of groups in the table.

The PR was measured with the adaptive SoftRecycler of #8698 cherry-picked onto it. Main does not include #8698, so the PR's columns combine both changes; its none column shows what #8698 alone does for random churn.

Findings

  • This branch without reclaiming (none) is 20% faster than main for random churn with one row per group when groups do not return, and 11% faster when they do; at ten million rows, 32% and 31%. Most of that comes from the adaptive recyclers of #8698, which main lacks: main's fixed recycler capacity turns away previous-value blocks that random churn needs again every cycle. Elsewhere it is within the error.
  • When groups do not return, every reclaiming mode is faster than main for the sliding window, by 32% to 34%, and retains about a quarter of its heap; at ten million rows, 26% to 27% faster with a sixth of the heap. For random churn, releasing blocks alone is 26% faster and retains a third less heap; collapsing is 21% to 23% faster and retains a third of the heap. At ten million rows, releasing blocks alone is 38% faster and collapsing 34% to 36%, and add only is 9% to 10% faster with reclaiming.
  • When groups return, the sliding window is slower with reclaiming, since returning groups are new states rather than reused ones: 12% releasing blocks alone, just inside the error, and 18% to 20% collapsing, and at ten million rows 10% to 12%. Random churn with returning groups is 13% slower releasing blocks alone and 17% to 20% slower collapsing at one million rows, but at ten million rows every reclaiming mode is 8% to 10% faster than main. Releasing blocks alone retains twice the heap of main there, and collapsing 3% to 15% less (at ten million rows, 2% more to 15% less).
  • For random churn at ten million rows, releasing blocks alone is 4% to 6% faster than collapsing when groups do not return, and within 2% of it when they do; at one million rows the modes are within 7% of each other. Collapsing retains far less heap.
  • Without the recycler changes (the branch at fb15638b78, with main's fixed recycler capacity), random churn with returning groups was 42% slower than main releasing blocks alone at one million rows and 23% slower at ten million.
  • No mode gives output positions back, so every reclaiming mode assigns the same positions: with one row per group, 10 million when groups do not return, and 8.71 to 10 million when they do, where main reuses 2 million; with 100 rows per group, 100,000, or 20,000 for random churn with returning groups.
  • With 100 rows per group, the modes are within the error of each other for time.
  • Reclaiming shortens the longest cycles when groups do not return: 18 to 26 ms for the sliding window and 11 to 23 ms for random churn, against main's 64 to 66 ms; at ten million rows, 93 to 102 ms and 86 to 134 ms, against 422 to 548 ms for main and main again. When groups return, the longest cycles are 11 to 18 ms for main and every mode, and 73 to 138 ms at ten million rows.
